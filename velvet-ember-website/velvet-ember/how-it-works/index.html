How a self-heating can works

Water plus calcium oxide makes heat. Velvet Ember keeps that reaction in a sealed chamber beneath the drink and lets the warmth pass up through an aluminium wall.

The short answer

A self-heating can has two sealed compartments. One holds the drink. The other holds calcium oxide and a separate pouch of water. When you activate the can, the water reaches the calcium oxide and they react to form calcium hydroxide, releasing heat. The heat moves through a metal wall into the drink, which never mixes with the reagents.

The chemistry is old and well documented. Engineers have used lime-and-water heaters in cans for decades. What makes or breaks a product is the engineering around it: how evenly heat spreads, how hot the outside gets, and how reliably the seals hold.

What happens inside, step by step

This describes the mechanism in the current engineering design. It is not the consumer instruction label, which will be finalised after safety testing.

  1. The base is pressed

    Pressing the rubber cap at the bottom flexes a plastic piercing pin upward.

  2. The water pouch is pierced

    The pin punctures the sealed 60 ml distilled-water pouch inside the lower chamber.

  3. Water meets calcium oxide

    The water soaks into the 69 g calcium oxide bed. The reaction releases heat and produces calcium hydroxide, which stays sealed inside the module.

  4. Heat moves into the drink

    Heat passes through the wall of the sealed aluminium cell into the hot chocolate above.

  5. You open and drink

    Once the drink has warmed, you open the pull-tab lid on top as you would any can.

Before you try one: always follow the instructions printed on the can. Final steps, waiting time and handling guidance are being validated with our engineering and safety testing, and will be published here before launch.

The chemistry, in one line

Calcium oxide + water → calcium hydroxide + heat (CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂). This is the same reaction that makes quicklime warm up when it’s wet, a property people have used for centuries.

Why calcium oxide?

It’s widely available, inexpensive, and needs only water to start reacting. The trade-off is that it adds weight and takes up space, which is why the heating chamber in Velvet Ember occupies the lower 190 ml of a 420 ml can.

Why aluminium?

Aluminium conducts heat well and is the standard material for drinks cans, so heat can travel from the sealed cell into the drink efficiently.
