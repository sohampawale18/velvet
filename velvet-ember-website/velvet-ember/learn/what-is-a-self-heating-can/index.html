What is a self-heating can?

A self-heating can is a container with its own heater: a sealed chamber that produces heat on demand and warms the food or drink beside it, no stove, kettle or electricity required.

By the Velvet Ember team. Published and updated 17 November 2026.

A self-heating can has two compartments: one for the drink and one for a heat-producing reaction. You start the reaction, usually by pressing or twisting the base, and heat flows through a metal wall into the drink. The reaction materials are designed to stay sealed away from what you drink.

How does a self-heating can produce heat?

Most consumer designs use an exothermic reaction, a chemical reaction that gives off heat. The most common pairing is calcium oxide (quicklime) and water: when they meet, they form calcium hydroxide and release heat. Some newer designs use solid-state reactions instead, which don’t need water at all.

In a can, the reagents sit in their own sealed module. Activating the can, for example by pressing a cap that pierces a water pouch, starts the reaction. The heat then moves by conduction through the module wall into the drink.

Is this a new idea?

No. The concept is more than a century old, and several large food and drink companies have trialled self-heating cans over the past few decades. A 2001 trial of a self-heated canned coffee in the UK and a 2005 self-heating latte in the US both relied on lime-and-water heaters. In the 2020s, US coffee brands have sold cans using a solid-state heater that warms in around two minutes after a twist of the base.

Why aren’t self-heating cans everywhere?

Because the engineering is genuinely difficult. The recurring challenges are:

  • Space: the heater takes up room, so there’s less drink than the can size suggests.
  • Even heating: drink nearest the heater warms first, so many designs ask you to shake or wait.
  • Cost: a heater adds parts and assembly, making each can more expensive than an ordinary one.
  • Surface temperature and seals: the outside must stay comfortable to hold and the module must stay sealed, which is why testing matters.
  • Disposal: a used can contains a spent heating module as well as metal, so recycling guidance has to be clear.

How does Velvet Ember approach it?

Velvet Ember uses the calcium oxide and water method. Its 420 ml can is split into a 230 ml hot chocolate section and a 190 ml lower chamber holding a sealed aluminium cell with 69 g of calcium oxide and a 60 ml water pouch. Heat-up time, serving temperature and safety results are still being validated, so we don’t quote them yet. See the current specifications.

What should you check before buying any self-heating drink?

  • The net volume of drink, not just the can size.
  • The stated heat-up time and how you’re asked to activate it.
  • Ingredients, allergens and the food-safety licence number on the label.
  • Handling warnings and how to dispose of the used can.
